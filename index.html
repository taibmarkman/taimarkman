I study how criminal enforcement shapes communities: how policing practices affect crime, how race affects outcomes at each stage of the criminal justice process, and what happens to people after they move through the system.

I am a pre-doctoral research fellow at Stanford Law School, working with Professor John Donohue on empirical criminal justice research. I am also the lead quantitative researcher on Racial Justice Act litigation with the Three Strikes Project, directed by Professor Michael Romano. My methods are modern causal inference for panel data, especially staggered difference-in-differences, along with randomized evaluations and item response theory.

I graduated from Georgetown University in 2025 with a B.A. in Political Economy. I am applying to economics Ph.D. programs for Fall 2027. Fields: applied microeconomics, public economics, economics of crime and discrimination, political economy, labor economics.

Papers

Crime on ICE: Local Police Behavior and Immigration Enforcement in the United States

with John Donohue and Baldwin. Working paper.

Abstract

A growing number of local police departments now hold authority to enforce federal immigration law, a role traditionally reserved for federal officers. Despite claims that this authority enhances public safety, empirical evidence remains limited. Using FBI Uniform Crime Report data from 2000–2024, we exploit staggered adoption across U.S. jurisdictions to estimate causal effects on policing. We find that this enforcement authority increases arrests for low-level, discretionary offenses by approximately 7%, with no corresponding decreases in offense rates or serious crime. These results suggest that such agreements realign police priorities toward discretionary enforcement without measurable public safety benefits.

Loaded Chambers: Organized Interests, Public Opinion, and Policy Responsiveness in the American States

with Iwasaki, Baldwin, and John Donohue. NBER Working Paper No. 34970.

Summary

We examine how state legislators respond to organized interests relative to constituent sentiment on gun policy, using an instrumental variables approach. I built the Gun Law Index used as the outcome measure and developed an item response theory and post-stratification approach to estimate new measures of gun sentiment in each state.

Ballots Beyond Bars: Understanding the Role of Information Costs in Voter Turnout for Those With Felony Convictions

Carroll Round Proceedings, Vol. 20 (2026): 177–201.

Abstract

In recent years, U.S. states have adopted a wide variety of legislation to re-enfranchise those with past felony convictions. However, a lack of transparency in these laws has resulted in increased information costs for individuals, complicating their understanding of voter eligibility. We conduct two large-scale randomized controlled trials in Pennsylvania to examine the effect of information campaigns on both registration and turnout. While our findings indicate a statistically significant increase in registration by 12%, we find no evidence that these campaigns enhanced voter turnout overall, highlighting persistent structural, informational, and direct barriers associated with voting for this historically underrepresented group. Our study provides actionable insight for optimizing get-out-the-vote and information campaigns to effectively minimize de facto disenfranchisement.

Work in progress

  • Federal regulation and prison conditions. Under the Prison Rape Elimination Act, states that do not certify compliance face a 5% reduction in prison-related funding. Using the timing of facility audits linked to national survey and administrative data, I test whether audits change facility conditions, recidivism, and earnings.
  • Pretextual stops. Using variation in stop-initiation policies, I ask whether limiting officer discretion reduces racial disparities or displaces them further into the legal process.
  • Race at each stage of the criminal process. Linking Racial Justice Act data to administrative case records, I study whether disparities at arrest and charging persist through plea bargaining and sentencing.
